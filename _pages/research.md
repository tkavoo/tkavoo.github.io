My research combines process-based land-surface modeling, environmental observations, and data science to improve predictions of ecosystem water and carbon cycling and to understand environmental change.

## Forest water and carbon cycles

I use the Community Land Model (CLM) to investigate how parameter uncertainty affects simulated carbon uptake and water loss. My master's research developed a point-scale parameter-optimization approach using observations from three National Ecological Observatory Network (NEON) flux-tower sites. The approach reduced gross primary production overestimation by 30% and evapotranspiration underestimation by 10% in percent bias.

My doctoral research extends this work to two temperate forest vegetation types across fourteen NEON sites. By testing whether site-level improvements transfer across locations and to regional scales, I aim to improve predictions of forest water demand and carbon uptake under environmental change.

## Coastal wetland salinity

In the Mobile–Tensaw River Delta, I use machine-learning models to reconstruct salinity dynamics and investigate environmental thresholds, vegetation responses, and coastal wetland vulnerability. This work supports a better understanding of where salinity is changing and how those changes affect freshwater forest ecosystems.

## Multi-model collaboration

Contributing to [CalLMIP](https://aimesproject.org/cal-lmip/), an international land-model comparison initiative to improve land surface model predictions.

## Approach

Across these projects, I develop reproducible workflows for environmental data processing, model simulations, and performance evaluation. My work connects model experiments with observational data and data-driven analysis to clarify sources of uncertainty and strengthen environmental predictions.
